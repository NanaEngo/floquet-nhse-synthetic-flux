Title: Two chains, one sensor: steering quantum noise away from the signal

Quantum sensors promise measurements of forces and fields with exquisite
sensitivity, but they face an uncomfortable truth: the very photons that
carry the signal also carry noise. In one-dimensional arrays of coupled
cavities, a curious effect called the non-Hermitian skin effect piles up
light at the array's edge. This pile-up boosts the response to a weak
signal, yet it amplifies quantum fluctuations just as strongly, so the
precision gained is given straight back.

We show how to break this deadlock with a simple architectural idea:
build two parallel chains whose directional transport runs in opposite
ways, and connect them weakly. The signal then grows along one chain
while quantum noise drains away down the other. Because no photon is
hidden from the accounting -- we count them in both chains -- the gain
is honest. An automatic optimization routine tunes all the couplings and
reveals two useful personalities: a "quiet" mode where each photon buys
nearly perfect precision but little signal throughput, and an "amplifier"
mode that boosts signals strongly in one direction while blocking the
reverse one. Both personalities survive small fabrication imperfections
when the design is re-tuned, and both are compatible with
superconducting-circuit and phononic-crystal platforms.

This turns a celebrated nuisance of non-Hermitian physics into a design
resource for precision force and field sensing.
